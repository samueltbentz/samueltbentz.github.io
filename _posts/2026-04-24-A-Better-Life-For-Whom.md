I first learned of the Boomer-aged author, Lionel Shriver, when a short video clip of hers came across my timeline in which she described her decision not to have children as “selfish”,  “ungenerous” and “irresponsible.” Taken aback by the self-reflective honesty, I was eager to find out more. As I learned, Shriver is an acclaimed writer and very well-known in the literary world. She recently released a new novel called, *A Better Life*, which earned the endorsement of negative reviews in the New York Times and The Atlantic.

Set during the migrant surge of the Biden administration, the book tells the story of a mid-twenties, American, white man named Nico. Nico lives at home with his divorced mother in a large Brooklyn house and doesn’t have a job, girlfriend or any ambition in his life. Instead, he is content to consume Right wing online media which is, according to his mother, radicalizing him. Nico’s mother, Gloria, on the other hand, is a stereotypical Liberal woman who typifies the progressive ethos.

In *A Better Life*, Shriver imagines a world where Mayor Eric Adams’ idea of paying New Yorkers to house migrants in their personal residences comes to fruition under the program name, “Big Apple, Big Heart” (this was an actual idea proposed by Mayor Adams that never realized). The book centers on Gloria’s decision to welcome a young Honduran migrant woman into their home, despite Nico’s strong opposition. The situation quickly escalates, leading to further chaos in the home.

A novel about immigration almost always would be told from the perspective of the migrant but Shriver uniquely tells the story from the perspective of the hosts. As a result, the impact of immigration on Nico and his family takes center stage and forces the reader to consider whether the family deserves sympathy for how their lives are affected. Shriver effectively criticizes the ingratitude of the migrants and her writing mocks and satirizes the ridiculousness of progressivism found in Gloria and Nico's two older sisters. Ultimately, though, *A Better Life* is not entirely dedicated to making fun of progressives, as fun as that would be.

Nico becomes a secondary and perhaps more subtle target of Shriver’s writing. He may hold correct political opinions and his skepticism of the migrants is often proven correct but Nico is functionally ineffectual and without agency. He lacks the ability or willingness to do something as fundamental as protect his own home from being overrun and invaded. Nico is without initiative and ambition and the book describes him at one point as wanting nothing. The arc of Nico in the story is not a change of mind but ultimately a progression from passivity to action. Once Nico's way of life is threatened, his natural instinct to protect his family and home begins to surface and develop. 

Nico may be a fictional character but his situation certainly is not. Nico resonates because Shriver capably illustrates how society has made young men like Nico expendable and pushed them towards inaction and resignation. In many ways, Nico’s passivity could be attributed to a lack of responsibilities in his life. As a result, Nico provides and produces nothing, owns nothing and is not needed by anybody. Before his house is overrun, he has nothing to defend, nothing to pass on and nobody to pass it on to. The immigration crisis limited opportunities for him in all facets of life and his response was to simply give up. A lack of hope for the future, for the prospect of a good life in one's own nation, can easily lead to stagnation and demoralization, or as Timon Cline recently [observed](https://americanreformer.org/2026/04/never-despair/), "despair."

It is unfortunately common for a young man in America today to not own anything of substance, to not be working or married, and without children. Without traditional mile-markers and societal norms pushing directionless young men along, they may begin to drift or simply opt out of life.  In his book on boyhood, Anthony Esolen observed what truly drives and motivates boys and men, “Boys need to be needed more than they need to be loved, or they need to be loved by being needed. It is indispensable to them that they should be dispensable, that is, needed to be spent up, for the women they love and the land they would protect, and, in the end, the faith they would affirm.”

Or in the case of our own Vice President, J. D. Vance, his development into manhood came when he was able to protect and provide for his family. As he wrote in his memoir, *Hillbilly Elegy*, “Paying for [Mamaw’s] health insurance made me feel, for the first time in my life, like I was the protector. It gave me a sense of satisfaction that I'd never imagined.” Vance goes on to tell a story of when he spent a day with his family upon coming home from a break while serving in the Marines, “I drove the whole way, I paid for gas, and I bought everyone dinner (admittedly at Wendy's). I felt like such a man, a real grown-up. To laugh and joke with the people I loved most as they scarfed down the meal that I'd provided gave me a feeling of joy and accomplishment that words can't possibly describe.” Sadly, many young men in America today have not experienced the joys that come from having the responsibility and agency to provide for and protect the people you love.

Men in America today need revitalization but what precedes revitalization is the opportunitiy of responsibilty. For men to have purpose and vitality, they need a positive vision, yes, but more than that they need people, institutions, businesses and communities that depend on them and their strength. The adage is true that with power comes responsibility but conversely with responsibility comes power. Men without responsibilities in their lives, without people to provide for and protect are powerless and vulnerable to resignation or despair. Jason Carroll recently made the point in a [study](https://ifstudies.org/blog/marital-horizons-the-motivating-power-of-marriage-for-young-men) at the Institue for Family Studies that married men "work harder not merely because marriage selects for productive men—but also because marriage gives men something worth working for." This may seem obvious for most married men or fathers, but the point stands: if a society wants more productive, virtuous men, it needs to encourage a way of life where there is something or someone(s) worth fighting for and show and communicate that the men are actually needed.

Men need to cultivate ambition, reject the attempts to have them pacified, and instead fight against the resignation that comes from societal decline. An American man ought to aim to live a life worth defending and where there is opportunity, assume and take on responsibiltieiss.

In return, our society needs to encourage opportunities of responsibility for men and offer a definition of manhood that extends beyond self-sacrifice and service, as Aaron Renn recently observed. Our nation will not be restored without the strength of men. Revitalization in America will only happen if Americans are unwilling to resign and give up, no matter the circumstances. A man with responsibilities and people to care for cannot afford to burn it all down. It is, as George Washington famously said, “conquer or die” for men with real responsibilities. That is why, in the end, the future of such men will in turn determine the future of our nation.
