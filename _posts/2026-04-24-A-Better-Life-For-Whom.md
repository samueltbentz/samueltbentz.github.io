I first learned of the Boomer-aged author, Lionel Shriver, when a short video clip of hers came across my timeline in which she described her decision not to have children as “selfish”,  “ungenerous” and “irresponsible.” Taken aback by the self-reflective honesty, I was eager to find out more. As I learned, Shriver is an acclaimed writer and very well-known in the literary world. She recently released a new novel called, *A Better Life*, which for political reasons, was reviewed very negatively by the New York Times and The Atlantic.

Set during the migrant surge of the Biden administration, the book tells the story of a mid-twenties white man named Nico. Nico lives at home with his divorced mother in a large Brooklyn house and doesn’t have a job, girlfriend or any ambition in his life. Instead, he is content to consume Right wing online media which is, according to his mother, radicalizing him. Nico’s mother, Gloria, on the other hand, is a stereotypical Liberal woman who typifies the progressive ethos and the notion of untethered empathy.

In *A Better Life*, Shriver imagines a world where Mayor Eric Adams’ idea of paying New Yorkers to house migrants in their personal residences comes to fruition under the program name, “Big Apple, Big Heart” (this was an actual idea proposed by Mayor Adams that never realized). The book centers on Gloria’s decision to welcome a young Honduran migrant woman into their home, despite Nico’s strong opposition.

The migrant, Martine, appears initially to be a welcome addition to the home as she devotes her time to cooking, cleaning and helping out around the house, unlike Nico. Martine quickly wins the favor of Gloria and Nico’s progressive two older sisters who live nearby in the city. However, over time and without explicit approval from Gloria, Martine doesn’t end up being the only migrant to start living at the house. The dynamic in the home quickly shifts and the house is ultimately overrun by foreigners.

The house in Brooklyn becomes a smaller picture of what was happening to America as a nation at that time. The story rarely ever leaves the home, highlighting the reality that Gloria and Nico don’t really have any other place to go, because after all, this was their home where they lived for years. The book is uniquely told from the perspective of the hosts rather than the migrants. This forces the reader to question whether the hosts deserve any sympathy for what is happening to their lives and their home. Many are often quick to emphasize the duty to welcome strangers and extend charity and mercy towards refugees and immigrants, however, Shriver's storytelling also illustrates the two-sided nature and exchange of hospitality. Good parents, for instance, teach their children how to be a guest in someone else’s home. The migrants in Gloria's home are ungrateful and ruthlessly take advantage of the kindess extended to them as guests. Similarly, America is having to now reckon with the reality that we have invited strangers into our home who are not upholding their duties as guests.

Shriver effectively criticizes the ingratitude of the migrants and her writing mocks and satirizes the ridiculousness of progressivism, found in Gloria and Nico’s sisters. The three women in the family are quick to believe anything Martine says and defend her actions, even to their own self-detriment. Gloria and her daughters cannot even seem to imagine that Martine could be manipulative, dishonest or capable of doing wrong.

However, the book is not entirely devoted to making fun of progressives, as fun as that would be. Nico becomes a secondary and more subtle target of Shriver’s writing. Without the father in the home, Nico is the de facto man of the house. He may hold correct political opinions and his skepticism of the migrants is often proven correct. But Nico is functionally ineffectual and without agency. He has no ability to do something as basic as protect his own home from being overrun and invaded. He lacks initiative and ambition and the book describes him as wanting nothing. The arc of Nico in the story is not as much a change of mind but a progression from passivity to action. Notably, this coming of age story doesn’t follow the typical timeline of a teenager during his formative years but rather as an adult in his mid-twenties. Shriver's choice of Nico's age highlights a real phenomenon affecting young men across America who are suceptible to resignation and delaying the beginning of their adult lives as a result.

Nico may be a fictional character but his situation certainly is not. Nico resonates because Shriver capably illustrates how society has made young men like Nico expendable and actually pushed them towards inaction and resignation. In many ways, Nico’s passivity could be attributed to his lack of responsibilities. This isn’t altogether unrelated to the immigration crisis which limits opportunities for him in all facets of life. This lack of hope for the future, for the prospects of a good life in his own nation, leads to stagnation and demoralization. It is not uncommon for a young man in America today to not own anything of substance, to not be working, married, and without children. As a result, Nico provides nothing, owns nothing and is not needed by anybody. Before his house is overrun, he has nothing to defend, nothing to pass on and nobody to pass it on to. In his book on boyhood, Anthony Esolen observed what drives and motivates boys and men, “Boys need to be needed more than they need to be loved, or they need to be loved by being needed. It is indispensable to them that they should be dispensable, that is, needed to be spent up, for the women they love and the land they would protect, and, in the end, the faith they would affirm.”

Or in the case of our own Vice President, J. D. Vance, his transition to manhood came when he was able to protect and provide for his family. As he wrote in his memoir, *Hillbilly Elegy*, “Paying for [Mamaw’s] health insurance made me feel, for the first time in my life, like I was the protector. It gave me a sense of satisfaction that I'd never imagined.” Vance goes on to tell a story of when he spent a day with his family upon coming home from a break while serving in the Marines, “I drove the whole way, I paid for gas, and I bought everyone dinner (admittedly at Wendy's). I felt like such a man, a real grown-up. To laugh and joke with the people I loved most as they scarfed down the meal that I'd provided gave me a feeling of joy and accomplishment that words can't possibly describe.” Sadly, many young men in America today have not experienced the joys that come from having the responsibility and agency to provide for and protect the people you love.

What men in America today need is revitalization. They need a positive vision, yes, but more importantly they need the opportunity of responsibility. The aim ought to be to live a life worth defending and protecting. The adage is true that with power comes responsibility but conversely with responsibility comes power. Men without responsibilities in their lives, without people to provide for and protect are powerless and vulnerable to resignation or despair. Shriver’s novel is ultimately effective because mass immigration is more than just a threat to constancy, safety and order. Fundamentally it invokes a primal and natural reaction to being invaded and conquered. Many try to suppress the instinctual response to protect and defend your own nation and family in the name of inclusion or empathy. But men need to reject the attempts to have them pacified, reject stagnation and instead fight against the resignation that comes from societal decline.

Men need responsibilities, ambition, and as Aaron Renn recently observed, a definition of manhood that extends beyond self-sacrifice and service. Our nation will not be restored without the strength of men. Revitalization in America will only happen if Americans are unwilling to resign and give up, no matter the circumstances. A man with responsibilities and people to care for cannot afford to burn it all down or be an accelerationist. It is, as George Washington famously said, “conquer or die” for men with real responsibilities. That is why, in the end, the future of such men will in turn determine the future of our nation.
