I first learned of the Boomer-aged author, Lionel Shriver, when a short video clip of hers came across my timeline in which she described her decision not to have children as “selfish”,  “ungenerous” and “irresponsible.” Taken aback by the self-reflective honesty, I was eager to find out more. As I learned, Shriver is an acclaimed writer and very well-known in the literary world. She recently released a new novel called, A Better Life, which for political reasons, was reviewed very negatively by the New York Times and The Atlantic.

Set during the migrant surge of the Biden administration, the book tells the story of a mid-20s white man named Nico. Nico lives at home with his divorced mother in a large Brooklyn house and doesn’t have a job, girlfriend or any ambition in his life. Instead, he is content to consume Right wing online content which is, according to his mother, radicalizing him. Nico’s mother, Gloria, on the other hand, is a stereotypical Progressive woman who typifies the progressive ethos and and the notion of untethered empathy

In A Better Life, Shriver imagines a world where Mayor Eric Adams’ idea of paying New Yorkers to house migrants in their personal residences comes to fruition under the program name, “Big Apple Big Hearts” (this was a real idea proposed by Mayor Adams that never realized). The book centers on Gloria’s decision to welcome a young Honduran migrant woman into their home, despite Nico’s strong opposition.

The migrant, Martine, appears initially to be a welcome addition to the home as she devotes her time to cooking, cleaning and helping out around the house, unlike Nico. Martine quickly wins the favor of Gloria and Nico’s progressive two older sisters who live nearby in the city. However, over time and without explicit approval from Gloria, Martine doesn’t end up being the only migrant to start living at the house. The dynamic in the house quickly shifts and the home is ultimately overrun by foreigners.

The house in Brooklyn becomes a smaller picture of what was happening to America as a nation at that time. The story rarely ever leaves the home, highlighting the reality that Gloria and Nico don’t really have any other place to go. The book is uniquely told from the perspective of the hosts rather than the migrants. This forces the reader to question whether the hosts deserve any sympathy for what is happening to their home. People are often quick to emphasize the duty to welcome strangers and extend charity and mercy, however, A Better Life also highlights the nature of hospitality that it is a two-sided exchange and there are in fact duties as a guest. Every good parent, for instance. teaches their children how to be a guest in someone else’s home. America is now having to reckon with the reality that illegal immigrants have not only entered illegally but have taken advantage of the kindness of America and robbed Americans of money, jobs and resources.

Shriver is able to adeptly criticize the ingratitude of the migrants as well as mock and satirize the ridiculousness of progressivism, found in Gloria and Nico’s sisters. The three women in the family are quick to believe anything Martine says and defend her actions, even to their own self-destruction. Gloria and her daughters cannot even seem to imagine that Martine could be manipulative or capable of doing wrong.

However, the book is not entirely devoted to making fun of progressives, as fun as that would be. Nico becomes a secondary and more subtle target of Shriver’s writing. Without the father in the home, Nico is the de facto man of the house. He may hold correct political opinions and his skepticism of the migrants is often proven correct. But Nico is functionally ineffectual and without agency. He has no ability to do something as basic as protecting his own home and family from being overrun and invaded. He lacks initiative and ambition and the book describes him as wanting nothing. The arc of Nico in the story is not as much a change of mind but a progression from passivity to action. Notably, this coming of age story doesn’t follow the typical timeline of a teenager during his formative years but rather as an adult in his mid-20s. Shriver highlights this delayed life and resignation phenomenon affecting young men across America.

Nico may be a fictional character but his situation certainly is not. Nico resonates because Shriver capably illustrates how society has made young men like Nico expendable and actually pushed them towards inaction and resignation. In many ways, Nico’s passivity could be attributed to his lack of responsibilities. Thisisn’t altogether disassociated from the immigration crisis which limits opportunities for him in all facets of life. This lack of hope for the future, for the prospects of a good life in his own home nation, leads to stagnation and demoralization. Young men in America today often don’t own anything of substance, aren’t working, aren’t married, don’t have children and are being ousted in the workforce by foreign laborers. As a result, he provides nothing, owns nothing and is not needed by anybody. Before his house is overrun, he has nothing to defend, nothing to pass on and nobody to pass it onto. He is expendable and replaceable. Anthony Esolen observed in his book on boyhood, “Boys need to be needed more than they need to be loved, or they need to be loved by being needed. It is indispensable to them that they should be dispensable, that is, needed to be spent up, for the women they love and the land they would protect, and, in the end, the faith they would affirm.”

Or in the case of our own Vice President, J. D. Vance, his transition to manhood came when he was able to protect and provide for his family. As he wrote in his memoir, Hillbilly Elegy, “Paying for [Mamaw’s] health insurance made me feel, for the first time in my life, like I was the protector. It gave me a sense of satisfaction that I'd never imagined” Vance goes on to tell a story of when he spent the day with his family after coming home on break from serving as a Marine, “I drove the whole way, I paid for gas, and I bought everyone dinner (admittedly at Wendy's). I felt like such a man, a real grown-up. To laugh and joke with the people I loved most as they scarfed down the meal that I'd provided gave me a feeling of joy and accomplishment that words can't possibly describe.”

Young men in America today need revitalization. They need a positive vision, yes, but more importantly they need the opportunity of responsibility. They ought to aim to live lives worth defending and protecting. The adage is true that with power comes responsibility but conversely with responsibility comes power. Men without responsibilities in their lives, without people to provide for and protect are powerless and susceptible to resignation. Shriver’s novel is ultimately effective because mass immigration is more than just a threat to constancy, safety and order. More fundamentally it invokes a primal and natural reaction to being invaded and conquered. Many try to suppress the instinctual response to protect and defend your own nation and family in the name of inclusion or empathy. But men need to reject the attempts to have them pacified and reject stagnation and instead fight against the resignation that comes from societal decline. A lack of energy and vitality in men will only further the decline. William Butler Yeats wrote, "The best lack all conviction, while the worst are full of passionate intensity." 

For the tide to turn, men need responsibilities, ambition and as Aaron Renn recently observed a definition of manhood that extends beyond self-sacrifice and service. Our nation will not be restored without the strength of men and an unwillingness of Americans to resign and give up, no matter the circumstances. A man with responsibilities and people to care for doesn’t have a blow everything up button option, he can’t afford to burn it all down or be an accelerationist. It is, as George Washington famously said, “conquer or die” for men with real responsibilities and that is why it will be those men who will lead.
